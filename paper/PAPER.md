## Abstract

After the 1996 welfare law, families receiving cash assistance per 100 poor families with children fell in every contiguous state, from an average of 68 in 1995–96 to 20 in 2015–16, unevenly. If social insurance shapes how voters respond to job loss, counties in states that lost more cash protection should have moved further toward the Republican presidential candidate when hit by the same local trade shock. The test uses public data and a design fixed before outcome data were downloaded. The outcome is the 2000–2016 change in the county Republican two-party share; the shock is commuting-zone exposure to Chinese import competition, instrumented as in earlier work; the moderator is the erosion of the state AFDC/TANF-to-poverty ratio, with state fixed effects. Across 3,106 counties the interaction is $-0.61$ points per standard deviation of erosion, with a 95% interval from $-1.56$ to 0.33: the sign opposes the hypothesis and the interval includes zero. Pre-period placebos are flat, and no pre-specified alternative moderator, control or shock measure yields significant amplification. The interval excludes amplification larger than about a quarter of the average shock effect per standard deviation. Translated into 2016 votes, it includes Democratic gains larger than the Republican margin in Michigan, Wisconsin and Pennsylvania and much larger losses, so county data cannot settle whether cash-assistance erosion changed those states. The main limit is the moderator: erosion mostly records how many families states aided in 1996, and the program reached few of the workers the shock displaced.

## Cash protection and the political response to job loss

In 1995–96 the average contiguous state aided about 68 families with Aid to Families with Dependent Children (AFDC) for every 100 poor families with children. In 2015–16, under Temporary Assistance for Needy Families (TANF), the average was 20, and the ratio had fallen in all 48 states. National accounting tells the same story. The Congressional Research Service estimates that 81.6% of AFDC-eligible individuals received benefits in 1995 and 26.6% of TANF-eligible individuals in 2016, and that cash assistance closed 27.3% of the aggregate poverty gap of families with children in 1995 against 5.7% in 2016 [@crs2019]. Over the same decades, commuting zones exposed to Chinese import competition lost manufacturing employment and drew more heavily on government transfers [@autor2013], and their counties shifted toward Republican presidential candidates [@autor2020].

These facts invite a question that the compensation literature has asked about other programs. Ruggie's account of embedded liberalism describes a postwar settlement in which liberal trade was made politically durable by domestic social protection [@ruggie1982], and Rodrik reads the association between openness and government size as a demand for insurance against external risk [@rodrik1998]. If social insurance shapes how voters respond to job loss, a state whose cash safety net eroded more after 1996 should show a larger Republican shift for the same local trade shock. This paper tests that prediction with public data and asks what the estimate implies for the 2016 result in Michigan, Wisconsin and Pennsylvania.

The closest evidence concerns programs aimed at trade-displaced workers. Margalit finds that between 2000 and 2004 the anti-incumbent effect of trade-related layoffs was smaller in counties where more workers were certified for Trade Adjustment Assistance [@margalit2011]. Kim and Pelc find that areas where that program responded least to import shocks shifted more toward the Republican candidate in 2016 [@kim2021psrm], and that successful petitions carried an electoral benefit for Democratic candidates [@kim2021cps]. A recent working paper by Balcazar and Lee uses the quasi-random assignment of petitions to officials to link processing delays to votes and attitudes against international integration [@balcazar2026]. In Western Europe, Swank and Betz report that universal welfare states weaken the link between openness and far-right support [@swank2003]. Halikiopoulou and Vlandas find that unemployment raises far-right support only where unemployment benefits or dismissal protection are low [@halikiopoulou2016], and their later individual-level work finds that several welfare policies reduce far-right voting among groups exposed to high risks [@vlandas2022]. Gingrich and Milner, in contrast, find that compensation does little to dampen the political response to automation and trade [@gingrich2019; @milner2021]. Fetzer links British welfare cuts after 2010 to support for the UK Independence Party and for Leave [@fetzer2019].

None of these studies asks whether state differences in the general, means-tested cash program for poor families moderated the presidential-vote response to the China shock. The test reported here does, with a design fixed in advance. The estimated interaction has the opposite sign to the hypothesis and an interval that includes zero; the pre-period placebos are flat; no pre-specified alternative supports amplification at conventional levels. Translated into votes, the interval for each of Michigan, Wisconsin and Pennsylvania includes changes larger than the 2016 margin in either direction, so the 2016 question lies below the resolution of this design.

## What the 1996 law changed

The Personal Responsibility and Work Opportunity Reconciliation Act, signed on August 22, 1996, replaced AFDC with block grants to states. Its new section 401(b) of the Social Security Act provides that the part "shall not be interpreted to entitle any individual or family to assistance under any State program funded under this part" [@prwora1996, 110 Stat. 2113]. Its section 408(a)(7) bars the use of federal funds for a family with an adult who has received federally funded assistance for 60 months [@prwora1996, 110 Stat. 2137]. The federal grant is fixed: \$16.5 billion a year, unadjusted at the national or state level since 1996, having lost 36% of its value to inflation since 1997 [@crs2019].

Other designs were on the table. The Work and Responsibility Act, introduced in the House on June 21, 1994, would have kept AFDC but redesigned it as time-limited, transitional assistance; states were to create temporary work positions for recipients who had received aid for 24 months [@hr4605, proposed sec. 492]. The Senate Democrats' Work First Act, introduced on August 3, 1995, would have repealed AFDC but appropriated "a sum sufficient" for its replacement [@s1117, proposed sec. 400], paid states the Medicaid matching percentage of their expenditures [@s1117, proposed sec. 413], and set a 60-month limit that did not count months in which a family lived in an area with unemployment above 7.5% [@s1117, proposed sec. 402(c)(1)]. President Clinton vetoed the Republican budget reconciliation bill, whose Title XII carried welfare provisions, on December 6, 1995 [@clinton1995veto], and the stand-alone welfare bill, H.R. 4, on January 9, 1996. The second veto message asked Congress to "protect States and families in the event of economic downturn and population growth" [@clinton1996veto, pp. 22–23].

The conference report on H.R. 3734 passed the House on July 31, 1996, by 328 to 101, with Republicans voting 230 to 2 and Democrats splitting 98 to 98 [@houserc383]. The Senate agreed to it the next day by 78 to 21; all 53 Republicans voted yea, and Democrats divided 25 to 21 [@senatevote262]. In signing it, the President objected that the Act "fails to provide sufficient contingency funding for States that experience a serious economic downturn" [@clinton1996signing, p. 1329].

What followed is documented in detail. Parolin decomposes the fall in cash assistance from 1993 to 2016 and attributes 52% of it to lower participation among income-eligible families, 21% to lower need and 27% to lower benefit levels [@parolin2021]. Bitler and Hoynes find that TANF did not respond to the Great Recession, while food stamps and unemployment insurance provided at least as much protection as in earlier recessions [@bitler2016]. The broader record is mixed: Blank's review finds that some of the new-style programs of the 1990s significantly increased work and earnings and reduced poverty [@blank2002], and aggregate welfare spending kept growing while shifting away from non-elderly, nondisabled single-parent families and the poorest families toward the elderly, the disabled, married parents and families with higher incomes [@moffitt2015]. The program that eroded served poor families with children: most AFDC assistance went to families headed by single mothers who reported no work [@crs2019], and TANF funds may not aid a family without a minor child or a pregnant member [@prwora1996, 110 Stat. 2134]. A displaced manufacturing worker could draw on it only as part of such a family.

## What earlier studies establish about politics

Exposure to Chinese imports, measured by commuting zone and instrumented with Chinese exports to other high-income countries, raised unemployment, nonparticipation and transfer receipt [@autor2013]. In the same framework, Autor, Dorn, Hanson and Majlesi estimate that each point of increase in Chinese import penetration between 2000 and 2008 raised the county Republican share of the two-party presidential vote between 2000 and 2016 by 1.71 points, with a standard error of 0.90 [-@autor2020, Table 5]. Related designs find that incumbent presidential vote shares fell in counties with many low-skilled manufacturing workers [@jensen2017], that manufacturing layoffs moved white voters toward Republican challengers and Black voters toward Democrats [@baccini2021], that counties exposed to NAFTA turned away from the Democratic Party [@choi2024], that exposure to permanent normal trade relations with China first helped Democrats [@che2022], and that Chinese import shocks raised support for Leave in Britain and for nationalist parties in Western Europe [@colantone2018apsr; @colantone2018ajps].

The policy-feedback literature explains why a welfare program might matter for votes. Policies supply resources and interpretive cues to the public as well as to elites [@pierson1993]; welfare clients learn lessons about government from program design [@soss1999]; and the size, visibility and administration of benefits shape participation and attitudes in either direction [@mettler2004; @campbell2012]. A quasi-experimental study of the 1996 reform and voting points away from demobilization: welfare reform reduced the decline in turnout among exposed women by about 3 to 4 percentage points between 1990 and 2004 [@corman2017]. Bisbee and coauthors trace the anti-globalist turn to disenchantment with the redistributive bargain between globalization's winners and losers [@bisbee2020].

Further results define the rivals. States' restrictive choices after 1996 were shaped especially by the racial composition of the families relying on benefits [@soss2001], so a state's later cash erosion may proxy for its racial politics, which also condition the political response to economic loss [@baccini2021]. And the economic reading of the 2016 vote is itself contested: Mutz attributes changing preferences to status threat over financial hardship, Morgan disputes the separation, and Margalit argues that economic insecurity explains a modest share of populist support even where it matters at the margin [@mutz2018; @morgan2018; @margalit2019].

## Data and design

The unit is the county, in the 1990-definition sample of Autor, Dorn, Hanson and Majlesi, excluding the District of Columbia, for which no ratio is published. The analysis sample has 3,106 counties in 48 states and 722 commuting zones. Election returns for 2000 to 2016 come from the MIT Election Data and Science Lab [-@medsl2018]; returns for 1988 to 2000, used for the placebo outcomes and the lagged controls, come from the county files of Algara and Amlani [-@algara2021data], documented by Amlani and Algara [-@amlani2021]. The shock, its instrument and the 2000 controls come from the replication archive of Autor, Dorn, Hanson and Majlesi [-@adhmdata2020]; the archive's own vote shares, drawn from a commercial atlas, enter only a validation exercise. The moderator comes from the state AFDC/TANF-to-poverty ratios of the Center on Budget and Policy Priorities [-@cbpp2025], which divide the number of families receiving cash assistance by the number of families with children in poverty, both as two-year averages. Benefit levels come from Table L5 of the 2016 Welfare Rules Databook [@wrd2017], deflated with the July consumer price index [@blscpi]; County Business Patterns supply an alternative shock [@cbp2000], aggregated to commuting zones with the county crosswalk of Autor and Dorn [-@autordorn2013]; and the Federal Election Commission [-@fec2017] supplies the official 2016 margins.

The outcome $\Delta R_c$ is the change in the Republican percentage of the two-party presidential vote in county $c$, from 2000 to 2016 in the primary specification and from 2012 to 2016 in the secondary specification. The shock $X_z$ is the change in Chinese import penetration in commuting zone $z$ from 2000 to 2008, in percentage points; across counties its interquartile range runs from 0.48 to 1.31. The moderator is the erosion of the state ratio, $M_s = \mathrm{TPR}_{1995\text{–}96} - \mathrm{TPR}_{2015\text{–}16}$, measured in families aided per 100 poor families. Across the 48 states erosion averages 47.6 points with a standard deviation of 19.0, from 13.1 in Oregon to 98.6 in Missouri. Michigan lost 74.4 points, Wisconsin 61.5 and Pennsylvania 56.9 (Figure 1a).

![State cash-assistance coverage and the trade-shock effect. (a) State AFDC/TANF-to-poverty ratios, 1995–96 against 2015–16 [@cbpp2025]; dashed line: equal coverage. (b) Effect of import penetration on the 2000–2016 change in the Republican two-party share, in points per point, by erosion: the pre-registered linear interaction with its two-way-clustered interval (line and band) and separate estimates for terciles of states ordered by erosion (points; descriptive, not pre-registered).](figures/fig1_moderation.png)

Erosion records where states started more than where they ended. Its correlation across states is 0.81 with the 1995–96 ratio and $-0.01$ with the 2015–16 ratio: by 2015–16 most states had converged on low coverage, and the states that lost most were those that had aided most. Benefit levels moved differently. The real maximum benefit for a family of three fell between July 1996 and July 2016 in 46 of the 48 states, by 24.5% on average, and its decline is nearly unrelated to the erosion of coverage, with a correlation of 0.13.

The specification is two-stage least squares, weighted by county total presidential votes in 2000:

$$\Delta R_c = \alpha_{s(c)} + \beta X_{z(c)} + \gamma\, X_{z(c)} \tilde M_{s(c)} + W_c'\delta + \varepsilon_c ,$$

where $\tilde M_s$ is erosion centred at its mean across states, $\alpha_s$ are state fixed effects that absorb the moderator's main effect, and $W_c$ holds the controls of the source design: the 2000 manufacturing employment share, routine-task share and offshorability index of the commuting zone; the county's 2000 population shares by sex, college education, foreign birth, age group and race or ethnicity; and the county Republican shares in 1992 and 1996. The shock and its interaction are instrumented by the exposure built from Chinese imports into eight other high-income countries [@autor2020, p. 3153] and its interaction with $\tilde M_s$. The coefficient $\beta$ is the shock effect at mean erosion, and the hypothesis predicts $\gamma > 0$.

The interaction is identified from differences in exposure between commuting zones within a state, compared across states that lost different amounts of cash protection. If the instrumented shock is as good as randomly assigned given the controls, $\gamma$ consistently measures how the shock effect varies with erosion even when erosion itself is correlated with omitted state traits [@nizalova2016; @bun2019]. Reading $\gamma$ as the effect of changing a state's cash protection requires the further assumption that erosion is not standing in for some other state characteristic that conditions the response to job loss. Standard errors are clustered jointly by state and by commuting zone [@cameron2011]; intervals and tests for state-clustered and two-way statistics use the $t$ distribution with 47 degrees of freedom. All intervals reported below are 95% confidence intervals. A wild cluster bootstrap by state, with Rademacher weights, the null imposed and 9,999 draws, checks the reduced-form interaction [@cameron2008]. Correlation of residuals across commuting zones with similar industry mixes, the concern of the shift-share inference literature, is not addressed by these standard errors [@adao2019; @borusyak2022].

The analysis plan, with the primary specification, the secondary window, the robustness checks, the falsification tests and the decision rule, was written into the project's brief and hashed before any election returns were downloaded. The deviations recorded there are the use of the laboratory's public mirror of the same election file, because the current repository copy sits behind a form that collects personal details; corrections to that file found by comparing it with an independent source and with official totals; the handling of a county created in 2001 in the business-pattern data; the degrees of freedom used for intervals; and descriptive additions, which are labelled as such where they appear.

## Results

### The open data reproduce the source design

With the open returns and the source design's division dummies, the 2000 to 2016 shock coefficient is 1.90 with a standard error of 0.92, against a published 1.71 and 0.90. Applied to the source design's own file, the same estimation code returns 1.71 with a standard error of 0.91. The gap therefore lies in the inputs. The weighted mean change in the Republican share is $-0.74$ points with a standard deviation of 9.97, close to the published $-0.74$ and 9.95 [@autor2020, Table 5 notes]. The county returns sum exactly to the official Trump and Clinton totals in Michigan and Pennsylvania; in Wisconsin they put Trump's lead at 22,617 votes, against 22,748 in the official returns. Replacing division dummies with state fixed effects, the shock effect without an interaction is 1.48, with an interval from $-0.78$ to 3.74.

### The interaction has the wrong sign and an interval that includes zero

The primary estimate of $\gamma$ is $-0.032$ per point of erosion, with an interval from $-0.082$ to 0.017 and a $p$-value of 0.20. Scaled by the cross-state standard deviation of erosion, it is $-0.61$, with an interval from $-1.56$ to 0.33: more erosion goes with a smaller trade-shock effect. At mean erosion the shock effect is 1.30, with an interval from $-0.88$ to 3.47. The implied effect is 1.72 at the 25th percentile of erosion and 0.85 at the 75th, a difference of $-0.87$ with an interval from $-2.19$ to 0.46. The instruments are strong. The state-clustered first-stage $F$ statistics are 53 for the shock and 85 for the interaction, and the Sanderson and Windmeijer conditional statistics are 107 and 97 [@sanderson2016]. Estimated separately for terciles of states ordered by erosion, the shock effect is 1.85 in the least-eroded tercile, 1.17 in the middle tercile and 0.87 in the most-eroded tercile (Figure 1b).

Other inference choices do not change the reading. Clustering by commuting zone alone gives an interval for $\gamma$ from $-0.069$ to 0.004 and a $p$-value of 0.08; clustering by state alone gives a $p$-value of 0.19; the wild cluster bootstrap for the reduced-form interaction gives 0.23.

The secondary window and the time profile point the same way (Table 1). The pre-period placebos do not show the pattern: the interaction for the change from 1992 to 1996 is $-0.03$ per standard deviation and that for 1996 to 2000 is 0.23, both with intervals that include zero. The plan's other placebo, the AFDC-era decline in the ratio from 1978–79 to 1995–96 used as the moderator, gives 0.19 per standard deviation, with an interval from $-1.05$ to 1.42.

### Robustness

![Interaction coefficients across pre-registered specifications, per cross-state standard deviation of each moderator, with intervals two-way clustered by state and commuting zone. The primary estimate is in blue; falsification tests are in red. Alternative moderators are oriented so that larger values mean less cash protection, so the hypothesis predicts positive coefficients throughout.](figures/fig2_robustness.png)

None of the pre-registered variants supports the hypothesis at the conventional level (Figure 2). Ordinary least squares gives $-0.51$ per standard deviation and the unweighted estimate $-0.29$. Adding interactions of the shock with the county white population share, a South indicator, the state's 1996 Republican share or the state's mean shock leaves the erosion interaction between $-0.66$ and $-0.62$; with all of them included it is $-0.88$, with an interval from $-2.08$ to 0.32. Dropping each state in turn moves $\gamma$ between $-0.045$, without New York, and $-0.016$, without California.

The alternative moderators disagree in sign, and none supports the hypothesis at the conventional level. Orienting each so that larger values mean less protection, the interaction per standard deviation is $-0.27$ for a low 2015–16 ratio, 0.15 for a low real 2016 benefit, $-0.14$ for a low 1996 benefit, and 1.32, with an interval from $-0.48$ to 3.12, for the percentage decline in the real benefit. The only significant estimate, for erosion measured to 2007–08 at the end of the shock window, is $-0.92$ with an interval from $-1.71$ to $-0.14$, in the direction opposite to the hypothesis. With six pre-specified moderators, a single estimate at this level is weak evidence.

The County Business Patterns shock, the decline in manufacturing employment between 2000 and 2008 as a share of 2000 employment, gives an interaction of $-0.0023$ per point of erosion by ordinary least squares, with an interval from $-0.0076$ to 0.0031. Instrumented with the trade exposure, its first stage is weak, with a state-clustered $F$ of 5.6, so that estimate carries little information.

### Precision

Given the estimated two-way standard error of 0.025, the design has 80% power, at the 5% level, only against an interaction of about 0.070 per point of erosion, or 1.34 per standard deviation: an amplification that would double the average shock effect for each standard deviation of erosion. The interval excludes amplification larger than 0.33 per standard deviation, about a quarter of the shock effect at mean erosion. Smaller amplification, plausible for a program that reached few displaced workers, is neither detected nor excluded.

## What the estimates imply for Michigan, Wisconsin and Pennsylvania

Taking $\gamma$ as the effect of erosion on the shock's political effect, the change in the Democratic-minus-Republican margin in state $s$ from setting its erosion to a counterfactual value $M^\ast$ is

$$\Delta\,\mathrm{margin}_s = \sum_{c \in s} 2\,\hat\gamma\, X_{z(c)} \left(M_s - M^\ast\right) \frac{V_c}{100},$$

where $V_c$ is the county's 2016 two-party vote, held fixed. The plan fixed the counterfactuals in advance: retaining the 1995–96 ratio ($M^\ast = 0$), which lies 13.1 points beyond the least-eroded state and is therefore an extrapolation, and the erosion of the least-eroded state, Oregon, which stays within the observed range. The interval for the margin change is the image of the interval for $\gamma$.

Under both counterfactuals the point estimates are Democratic losses, and the intervals include Democratic gains larger than Trump's 2016 margin in each state, as well as losses several times larger (Table 2 and Figure 3). The value of $\gamma$ that would have reversed each state under the first counterfactual is 0.0018 in Michigan, 0.0054 in Wisconsin and 0.0071 in Pennsylvania, all smaller than the standard error of the estimate and well inside its interval, whose upper end is 0.017. Trump's margins in Michigan, Wisconsin and Pennsylvania sum to 77,744 votes [@fec2017].

## What the test does and does not show

The test rules out a strong version of the hypothesis: an amplification of the presidential-vote response to the China shock larger than about a quarter of the average effect per standard deviation of erosion, in the counties and years studied. The point estimates in every pre-registered window lean the other way. The secondary window, which isolates the change from 2012 to 2016, shows no amplification either.

Several features of the moderator limit what a null can say about cash protection itself. Erosion of the ratio is driven by where states began in 1996, so the test compares states that had aided many poor families with states that had aided few, and such states also differ in region, racial composition and partisanship. The program that eroded served poor families with children, and displaced manufacturing workers could draw on it only as members of such families. In the source design's own accounting, federal income assistance, of which TANF is a part alongside Supplemental Security Income and food stamps, rose by \$7 per capita for each \$1,000 of import exposure per worker, against \$8 for disability insurance and \$18 for medical benefits [@autor2013, pp. 2148–2149]. A safety-net channel for the trade shock is more likely to run through unemployment and disability insurance than through TANF. The negative estimate for erosion to 2007–08 and the positive, imprecise estimate for the decline in real benefits show that different measures of the same retrenchment do not tell a single story.

The interaction measures how the shock effect varies with erosion, and the bridge to 2016 reads it as a causal effect of erosion. That reading fails if erosion proxies for racial composition, region or partisanship in ways the competing interactions do not capture, a concern the history of state policy choices makes concrete [@soss2001]. The bridge also holds turnout and the composition of the two-party vote fixed and ignores spillovers across counties. The standard errors do not account for correlation across commuting zones with similar industry mixes, which would widen the intervals. And the outcome is a county vote share, which cannot show which voters moved.

A sharper test would follow displaced workers and their households, linking the cash, unemployment and disability benefits they could claim under their state's rules to their later turnout and vote, or would use a moderator built from unemployment-insurance replacement rates, the program that reaches the workers the shock displaced. On the evidence here, the post-1996 erosion of cash assistance did not measurably amplify the county presidential-vote response to the China shock, and estimates at the county level cannot decide whether a smaller amplification changed the outcome in Michigan, Wisconsin or Pennsylvania.

## Code and data availability

All code, the analysis plan and the recorded execution receipt are at https://github.com/piatra-institute/papers-after-afdc. The script `simulation/fetch_data.py` downloads every input and checks it against a pinned SHA-256 hash; `simulation/run_all.py` reproduces every number, table and figure from those files. Raw downloads stay out of the repository and are re-fetched by that script. The repository includes the transcription of Table L5 of the Welfare Rules Databook (public domain), the fetch log and the aggregate results.

The inputs and their terms are as follows. MIT Election Data and Science Lab, County Presidential Election Returns 2000–2016 (doi:10.7910/DVN/VOQCHQ; CC0 1.0; obtained from the laboratory's GitHub copy, commit f44e3fc). Algara and Amlani, county presidential returns 1868–2020 (doi:10.7910/DVN/DGUMFI, version 1.1; CC0 1.0). Autor, Dorn, Hanson and Majlesi, replication archive from David Dorn's data page, and the 1990 county to commuting zone crosswalk; public downloads without a stated licence, used for the shock, the instrument and the controls and not redistributed. Center on Budget and Policy Priorities, state AFDC/TANF-to-poverty ratios (spreadsheet of April 11, 2025, retrieved from the Internet Archive because the publisher's site refuses automated clients); the publisher's terms permit noncommercial reproduction with attribution and bar derivative databases, so the spreadsheet is not redistributed, and values derived from it appear only in Figure 1, in summary statistics and for the states named in the text. Welfare Rules Databook, OPRE Report 2017-82 (public domain). County Business Patterns 2000 and 2008, Federal Election Commission Federal Elections 2016, and the Bureau of Labor Statistics consumer price index (US government works, public domain).
